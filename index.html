Hello there! 👋 Today, we are going to take a journey through one of the most fascinating processes in biology—childbirth. We are going to look at the Stages of Labour.

Imagine this as a story with four distinct chapters. I’ll break it down step-by-step so you can visualize exactly what happens.

The First Stage

Let’s begin with The First Stage. Think of this as the "opening up" phase.

Technically, we define this as the period from the onset of true, regular uterine contractions until the full dilatation of the cervical Os.

In simple terms? 🚪 It’s the time from when the labour pains really start until the door (the cervix) is fully open for the baby to come out. This stage is actually divided into two phases.

1. The Latent Phase

This is the slow start. It’s the period before things get very active. It starts from the spontaneous onset of labour until the cervix is about 3 to 4cm dilated.

During this time, the cervix also shortens—going from about 3cm long down to 0.5cm long. 📏

⏱️ How long does it last?
For a first-time mother (Primip), it might last 6 to 8 hours. However, for mothers who have given birth before (Multiparous), it is usually much shorter.

📉 Note: If you looked at a "partogram" (a chart used to track labour), the line remains horizontal during this phase because things are moving slowly.

2. The Active Phase

Okay, let’s move on to when things speed up! 🚀

This is the period from 3-4cm up to 10cm dilatation. The cervix undergoes much more rapid dilatation, opening at a rate of about 1cm per hour.

On the chart, the line now rises rapidly. This usually lasts 2 to 6 hours, but again, it’s faster for women who have given birth before.

Okay, now that the door is fully open (10cm dilated), let's talk about getting the baby out.

The Second Stage

This is the stage of Expulsion of the Foetus. 👶

It starts from the moment of full dilatation of the cervix and the urge to push, and it ends when the foetus is completely expelled from the mother's body.

The Third Stage

Next, we look at what happens immediately after the baby is born. This stage is all about the Placenta.

This is the period of separation and expulsion of the placenta and membranes, and the control of bleeding. 🩸

So, it lasts from the birth of the baby until the delivery of the placenta. Interestingly, the duration of this stage is not affected by parity (meaning it doesn't matter if it's the first baby or the fifth, this stage takes about the same time).

The Fourth Stage

You might think, "Wait, isn't it over?" Not quite! 🛑

The Fourth Stage is the first one hour following the birth of the placenta. It is actually the very first hour of the puerperium.

Why do we give this hour its own stage? To emphasize the importance of continuous vigilance. We need to watch the woman closely for the risk of Post Partum Haemorrhage (heavy bleeding). Just like the third stage, this timeline is not affected by how many babies she has had.

Let's now dive into the numbers. How long does all of this actually take?

Duration of Labour

There are wide variations here. The length of labour is influenced by many things:

  • Parity (number of previous births)
  • Time of the last delivery
  • Type of pelvis
  • Size and presentation of the foetus
  • Strength and frequency of uterine contractions

Generally, the greatest part of labour is taken up by the First Stage. But remember, the most important thing is simply that labour progresses, provided the woman is comfortable and the baby is well.

Labour lasts longer in Primigravidae (first-time moms) than in Multigravidae (experienced moms). Let's look at the average breakdown:

👩 First-Time Mom (Primigravida)

1st Stage: 8-12 hours
2nd Stage: 30-60 mins
3rd Stage: 5-15 mins

Average Total: ~12 Hours

👩‍👧‍👦 Experienced Mom (Multipara)

1st Stage: 6 ½ - 8 hours
2nd Stage: 15-30 mins
3rd Stage: 5-15 mins

Average Total: ~7 Hours

💡 Did you know? Evidence has shown that the use of Oxytocin and one-to-one care has reduced the period of labour considerably.

How to Calculate Duration

Let’s try a practical example together so you can see how the math works. 🧮

The Scenario:
Date: 12/6/2008
Labour begins: 12:30 a.m.
OS fully dilated: 7:15 a.m.
Baby delivered: 7:25 a.m.
Placenta delivered: 7:33 a.m.

The Calculation:

  • 1st Stage: 12:30 a.m. to 7:15 a.m. = 6 hrs 45 mins
  • 2nd Stage: 7:15 a.m. to 7:25 a.m. = 10 mins
  • 3rd Stage: 7:25 a.m. to 7:33 a.m. = 8 mins

TOTAL DURATION = 7 hrs 3 mins

Now that we understand the timing, let's talk about care. Specifically, how do we handle the pain?

Management of First Stage: Pain Relief

Pain is tricky. It is not possible to assess exactly how much pain a person is feeling because pain cannot be objectively measured. It's subjective.

However, we know that pain leads to physical and emotional exhaustion and lessens the woman’s confidence. Since the pain threshold varies from person to person, the woman in labour must be relieved from pain, while ensuring the baby's safety.

What causes the pain?

In labour, pain is caused by uterine contractions, dilatation of the cervix, and the stretching of the vagina and pelvic floor muscles (especially in the late 1st and 2nd stage).

⚡ These pains are transmitted by the thoracic, lumbar, and sacral nerves.

Methods of Pain Relief

1. Psychological Method

This is arguably the most important aspect. 🧠

A woman who is scared or apprehensive will feel more pain. If she is admitted into a clean, well-organized, calm, and reassuring environment, she will relax. The midwife must be sympathetic and understanding.

The midwife's personality should reflect kindness. This includes:

  • Giving information as necessary.
  • Allaying anxiety.
  • Participating in planning and care.
  • Giving physical care.

2. The Use of Drugs (Chemotherapy)

Midwives use various drugs to relieve pain, allay apprehension, or induce sleep. 💊

A small dose of a narcotic might act as a sedative, while a large dose of a tranquilizer might act as a hypnotic. The midwife must understand the underlying principles! Success depends on the right drug, the right dose, and proper timing.

Analgesics: Drugs supposed to relieve pain without knocking the patient out (e.g., Panadol, Aspirin, Fortral).

Narcotics: Strong analgesics that allay anxiety and induce sleep (e.g., Pethidine, Morphine, Pethilorfan, Tramal).

Hypnotics: These induce sleep and act as anti-convulsants (e.g., Chloral hydrate, Diazepam, Omnopon, Paraldehyde).

Tranquillizers: These calm the patient (e.g., Phenergan).

Sedatives: These induce sleep (e.g., Barbiturate groups).

🍹 The Lytic Cocktail

This refers to a mixture usually given intravenously (IV). It is a mix of phenothiazine derivatives and Pethidine.

Example Recipe:
Chlorpromazine (Largactil) 50mg + Promethazine (Phenergan) 50mg + Pethidine 100mg.

It is mixed and given slowly until the patient enters a state of sedation, tranquility, and analgesia (ataralgesia).

Uses: Treatment of pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, forceps deliveries, breech deliveries, and C-sections.

3. Inhalational Analgesia

Permitted by the Midwife Board for healthy women in the late first stage or second stage. These are volatile agents excreted quickly by the body. 💨

Entonox: Pre-mixed Nitrous Oxide (50%) and Oxygen (50%).

Trilene (Trichloroethylene): A blue liquid that evaporates into a non-inflammable vapour. It is administered using an Emotril Automatic Inhaler.

4. Obstetric Anaesthesia

Anaesthesia means the absence of sensation. There are three main types used:

  • General Anaesthesia: Total unconsciousness.
  • Regional Anaesthesia: Numbing a large region (e.g., Epidural, Spinal).
  • Local Anaesthesia: Numbing a small specific area (e.g., Lignocaine).

Let's look at the specific regional/local types briefly:

💉 Spinal Anaesthesia: Local anaesthetic is injected into the subarachnoid space (into the CSF).

💉 Pudendal Block: Injected adjacent to the pudendal nerves (just below the ischial spines). This numbs the pelvic floor, vulva, and perineum.

💉 Paracervical Block: This blocks the paracervical plexus. Used in prolonged labour. 10mls of 1% lignocaine is injected into the lateral fornices. ⚠️ Warning: It reduces backache but there is a risk of bradycardia (slow heart rate) and foetal death due to spasm of uterine vessels.

💉 Local Anaesthesia: 10mls of 0.5% Lignocaine infiltrated into the perineum, often used for episiotomy.
